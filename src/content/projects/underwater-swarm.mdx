## The problem

Underwater is the hardest place to run a robot swarm. You lose the things
terrestrial multi-robot systems quietly depend on: no GPS, no reliable
high-bandwidth radio, no shared clock, and severely limited communication range.
Anything that assumes a robot knows where it is, or can talk to the whole group,
is off the table.

The question the lab was asking: can a group of robots that can only exchange
very small messages with immediate neighbours still reach a *collective*
decision - detect an event, agree that it happened, and act on it - with no
central coordinator?

That question ran on two tracks at once, and they were the same project. One was
theoretical, and became my doctoral thesis. The other was a fleet of robots in a
lagoon.

## One paradigm rather than a catalogue

Swarm robotics has a proliferation problem. The literature contains a long list
of bio-inspired algorithms - each modelled on a different organism, each
presented with its own vocabulary, each demonstrating some collective behaviour.
Aggregation here, foraging there, collective decision-making somewhere else.

The thesis started from a suspicion: that many of these are not actually distinct
algorithms. They are the same underlying mechanism, parameterised differently and
described in the language of whichever organism inspired them.

So I developed a design paradigm intended to unify these behaviours under a
single formulation built on **minimalistic communication** - robots exchanging
very small amounts of information with immediate neighbours only. The claim being
tested was that known collective behaviours could be recovered as special cases
of one framework rather than requiring bespoke algorithms. It was published as
*A swarm design paradigm unifying swarm behaviors using minimalistic
communication* in **Bioinspiration & Biomimetics** (2020), and the doctorate was
completed under Prof. Franz Wotawa and Prof. Thomas Schmickl at TU Graz.

The framework deliberately did not stop at simulation, and that is where the two
tracks meet. A unification claim is only interesting if it survives contact with
real robots, where the idealised assumptions behind most swarm models - reliable
communication, synchronous updates, homogeneous agents - quietly fail. subCULTron
supplied the robots that could falsify it.

## From framework to firmware

I designed and implemented these algorithms in simulation and then ported them
down to embedded C/C++ running on the physical robots - sensing, inter-robot
communication and actuation modules. That full-stack path from behaviour model to
firmware is where most of the interesting failures live: a rule that converges
cleanly in simulation can be destroyed by a message-loss rate the simulator never
modelled.

Two further results came out of this line of work: **collective event detection
using bio-inspired minimalistic communication** (ALIFE 2019), and **CIMAX**, on
collective information maximization using local communication (Adaptive Behavior,
2020).

## Field deployment

The behaviours were validated in real deployments in the **Venice lagoon** - a
site with genuinely adversarial conditions for this class of system: turbidity,
currents, and communication characteristics that no lab tank reproduces.

Field robotics is a different discipline from lab robotics. The constraints stop
being algorithmic and start being logistical: what you can debug from a boat,
what you can recover if it fails, how many runs you get per day.

## Why it matters

Minimalistic-communication swarms are a useful stress test for a broader question
in multi-robot autonomy: how much coordination can you get from how little
information? The constraints that make underwater hard - partial observability,
unreliable communication, no global state - are the same constraints that show up
in any decentralised multi-agent system operating in the real world.

The through-line into everything I have built since is a preference for **one
well-chosen abstraction over many special cases**. The same instinct that asked
whether a dozen swarm algorithms were really one algorithm is the instinct that
later shaped how I drew the localization interface on a production robot: find
the boundary that makes the variations parameters rather than rewrites.
