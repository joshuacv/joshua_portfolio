## The problem

Underwater is the hardest place to run a robot swarm. You lose the things
terrestrial multi-robot systems quietly depend on: no GPS, no reliable
high-bandwidth radio, no shared clock, and severely limited communication range.
Anything that assumes a robot knows where it is, or can talk to the whole group,
is off the table.

The question the lab was asking: can a group of robots that can only exchange
very small messages with immediate neighbours still reach a *collective*
decision — detect an event, agree that it happened, and act on it — with no
central coordinator?

## Approach

The answer we pursued was bio-inspired and deliberately minimalistic. Rather than
building a consensus protocol, the robots ran simple local rules loosely modelled
on collective behaviour in biological systems, and the group-level decision
emerged from the interaction pattern rather than being computed anywhere in
particular.

I designed and implemented these algorithms in simulation and then ported them
down to embedded C/C++ running on the physical robots — sensing, inter-robot
communication and actuation modules. That full-stack path from behaviour model to
firmware is where most of the interesting failures live: a rule that converges
cleanly in simulation can be destroyed by a message-loss rate the simulator never
modelled.

Two results came out of this line of work: **collective event detection using
bio-inspired minimalistic communication** (ALIFE 2019), and **CIMAX**, on
collective information maximization using local communication (Adaptive Behavior,
2020).


## Field deployment

The behaviours were validated in real deployments in the **Venice lagoon** — a
site with genuinely adversarial conditions for this class of system: turbidity,
currents, and communication characteristics that no lab tank reproduces.

Field robotics is a different discipline from lab robotics. The constraints stop
being algorithmic and start being logistical: what you can debug from a boat,
what you can recover if it fails, how many runs you get per day.

Visibility collapses within a couple of metres, the units drift on the current,
and the water column looks the same in every direction — which is precisely why
none of the coordination could depend on a robot knowing where it was.

## Why it matters

Minimalistic-communication swarms are a useful stress test for a broader question
in multi-robot autonomy: how much coordination can you get from how little
information? The constraints that make underwater hard — partial observability,
unreliable communication, no global state — are the same constraints that show up
in any decentralised multi-agent system operating in the real world.
