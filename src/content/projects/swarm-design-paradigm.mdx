## The question

Swarm robotics has a proliferation problem. The literature contains a long list
of bio-inspired algorithms — each modelled on a different organism, each
presented with its own vocabulary, each demonstrating some collective behaviour.
Aggregation here, foraging there, collective decision-making somewhere else.

The thesis started from a suspicion: that many of these are not actually distinct
algorithms. They are the same underlying mechanism, parameterised differently and
described in the language of whichever organism inspired them.

## Approach

I developed a design paradigm intended to unify these behaviours under a single
formulation built on **minimalistic communication** — robots exchanging very
small amounts of information with immediate neighbours only. The claim being
tested was that known collective behaviours could be recovered as special cases
of one framework rather than requiring bespoke algorithms.

The work was done under Prof. Franz Wotawa and Prof. Thomas Schmickl, and
deliberately did not stop at simulation. A unification claim is only interesting
if it survives contact with real robots, where the idealised assumptions behind
most swarm models — reliable communication, synchronous updates, homogeneous
agents — quietly fail.

## Why this shaped how I build systems

The through-line from this work into everything I have built since is a
preference for **one well-chosen abstraction over many special cases**. The same
instinct that asked whether a dozen swarm algorithms were really one algorithm is
the instinct that later shaped how I drew the localization interface on a
production robot: find the boundary that makes the variations parameters rather
than rewrites.
