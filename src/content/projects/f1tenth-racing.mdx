## The platform

F1TENTH is a 1/10-scale autonomous racing platform: a real vehicle, with real
dynamics, that has to perceive, localize, plan and control entirely on board.
Scaling down removes the cost and safety envelope of full-size autonomous
driving without removing the hard part. The car still slides, the compute budget
is still finite, and the control loop still has to close in time or the car
leaves the track.

Ours runs **ROS 2 on a Jetson AGX Xavier**, sensing the world through a **single
2D lidar**. No camera.

That last constraint shapes everything. With no visual input, the entire
perception and localization stack is geometric - every decision the car makes is
derived from a planar scan. It removes an enormous class of problems (lighting,
exposure, domain shift) and replaces them with a sharper one: all the
information you will ever have is a ring of range measurements, so the
algorithms working on it have to be good. We used particle filter to localize, numerical 
optimization to calculate the optimal race line, a cubic spline based trajectory generator to 
calculate collision avoidance paths and model predictive control for acuation. 

## My role

I led the project as **team lead and lead developer**, responsible for the
hardware and the software, with minor contributions (hardware building) from teammates. In practice
that meant owning the whole vertical: vehicle build and wiring, sensor
integration, the ROS 2 stack, the controller, and the trackside decisions during
race weekend.

## From Nav2 to a custom MPC

The first working system used **Nav2**. That was a deliberate choice - it gets a
vehicle driving autonomously quickly, and it provides a known-good reference to
measure against. For bring-up and early testing it was the right tool.

It was not the right tool for racing because of its dependence on very good odometry. 
Nav2 is engineered to reach a goal *safely* in an environment shared with people and obstacles: conservative
velocity profiles, generous clearances, reactive replanning. Racing inverts
almost every one of those priorities. You want minimum-time trajectories, you
want to carry speed through corners, and you want to operate close to the limits
of tyre grip rather than comfortably inside them.

So the racing stack runs a **custom MPC** instead, closing the loop at **50 Hz**.
Model predictive control is the natural fit: it optimizes over a horizon against
an explicit vehicle model subject to explicit constraints, which is exactly the
shape of the racing problem. The engineering difficulty is that it has to solve
that optimization to convergence, every 20 milliseconds, on an embedded GPU
platform, without ever missing a deadline.

The MPC went from integration to **race-ready in two weeks**, and ran in
competition at the end of it. That timeline is the part I am most willing to be
judged on. Replacing the controller at the centre of a working autonomy stack is
the change most likely to break everything downstream of it, and doing it against
a fixed external deadline removes the option of extending the schedule until it
feels safe. It worked because the interfaces around the controller were drawn
well enough that swapping what sat inside them did not require renegotiating the
rest of the stack.

## Racing as a constraint

Competition is a useful forcing function. A stack that works in the lab has
soft deadlines; a stack that races has hard ones, and it has to survive
conditions you did not choose - an unfamiliar track, limited practice time, and
no opportunity to retune between runs.

## Result

The team placed **10th at ICRA 2026 in Vienna** - its first F1TENTH competition,
with a stack built from scratch.

## Where this goes next

The platform is the reason this project matters beyond the result. A Jetson
Xavier closing a 50 Hz loop against a hard deadline is a near-ideal testbed for
the question I am most interested in now: **how do you get modern learned models
fast enough to sit inside a real control loop?**

The obvious next step is to bring learned components into this stack - and to do
it under the same constraint that governs everything else here, which is that
the answer has to arrive before the next control cycle.
